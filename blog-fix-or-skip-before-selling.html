Fix It or Skip It? What to Do Before You Sell Your House

Before you spend thousands getting your house ready to sell, know which problem that money is solving. A simple way to sort every project, and what to leave alone.

October 1, 2026 Orion Love 4 min read

Before you spend thousands of dollars getting your house ready to sell, you need to know exactly which problem that money is solving. A dated kitchen, a leaking sink, and a cluttered bedroom are three completely different problems: one you can leave alone, one you have to deal with, and one that costs almost nothing to fix. Spend your money on the right one, and you’ll spend a lot less of it overall.

Why “getting ready to sell” turns into a remodel

The minute most people decide to sell, their house turns into a to-do list. You’ve lived there for years and stopped seeing it. Then suddenly you notice everything: the chipped paint, the worn carpet, the bathroom you’ve wanted to redo since the day you moved in. That’s how getting ready to sell can turn into a renovation you wouldn’t have touched otherwise. Before you start spending, it helps to separate the projects that actually help you sell from the ones that just bother you.

The three categories: repairs, presentation, or updates

Every project on a pre-sale list falls into one of three categories, and each one gets a different answer.

Repairs are things that aren’t working properly, like a leaky sink or a door that won’t latch. Presentation is how the house looks and feels when buyers walk through: deep cleaning, replacing old bulbs, clearing furniture to open up a room. Updates are things you’d change simply because they look dated, like an older kitchen or bathroom.

Start with any obvious repairs or anything that could read as neglect. Then tackle cleaning and presentation, usually the cheapest thing you can do. Before spending money on updates, think hard about whether you’d even get that money back. The seller FAQ has short answers on repairs, staging and what selling costs in Colorado.

Fix it now, or let the inspector find it?

A lot of sellers plan to skip small repairs and let the buyer’s inspector find them. That usually costs more in the long run. A buyer doesn’t know your house the way you do. They don’t know the back door has stuck for ten years, or that the ceiling stain was fixed years ago. One unexplained issue is just an annoyance; a handful of them, and a buyer starts wondering what else they’re not seeing. Then the inspection report lists the loose cabinet door and the dead outlet right alongside any real issues, and it all reads as one picture of the house. Fixing the small stuff ahead of time means those line items never make the report, and you fix it on your own schedule, not the buyer’s deadline.

A pre-listing inspection can be worth paying for, especially for an older home, a former rental, or one where you’re honestly not sure what’s going on behind the walls. It shows you what a buyer’s inspector is likely to find while you still have time to act. Just know that whatever it turns up becomes something you’ll need to address in your seller disclosures.

Big-ticket items: roof and furnace

An older roof or furnace doesn’t automatically need replacing before you sell. Age matters, but condition matters more. A furnace that’s working fine is a different situation than one that keeps breaking down. Start with the right professional, have them check its actual condition, and weigh that against your timeline and pricing. You don’t have to replace everything that’s old, but you should understand its condition before deciding.

The low-cost fixes buyers notice most

Sometimes the best thing you can do for a room is take something out of it. A king bed, two dressers, a desk, and boxes can make even a good-sized room read as “the small bedroom.” Pull a dresser and the boxes, and the room reads as a real bedroom again. The same goes for kitchen counters: buyers can’t see the workspace they’re shopping for if every surface is covered.

When cleaning before you list, focus on the details buyers notice up close, like faucets, baseboards, and mineral buildup on shower glass. Give the front door extra attention, since it’s often a buyer’s first few seconds standing still with nothing to do but look. And if your house has a smell, you’re probably the last person who can tell. Pets, smoking, or dampness all fade into the background when you live with them every day. Air freshener doesn’t fix the source; finding it does.

Outside, a quick walk-up as a buyer would see it (pulling weeds, trimming the walkway, sweeping the entry) goes a long way without a full landscaping redo. Inside, replacing burned-out bulbs and matching light color room to room can change how a space feels more than any new fixture would.

Dated isn’t defective

An outdated kitchen or bathroom isn’t automatically a problem. If it works, buyers will often accept it, as long as the rest of the home and the price make sense. The better question to ask is what buyers are seeing in the other homes they could buy instead. If you’re really considering a kitchen update, compare three real options side by side: a light refresh, a full renovation (including the time it takes and the chance it grows), and selling as-is, priced for its condition. Often, the answer is a lot less work than a full remodel.

Fix the repairs, handle the presentation, and think hard before paying for updates. Knowing which category a project falls into decides whether it’s worth doing before you list.

Talk to an agent before you start

You don’t need your house to be picture-ready before inviting an agent over. An early walkthrough is one of the most useful steps in the whole process, and it’s where my seller process starts. Looking at condition, your real competition, your budget, and your timeline together builds a short list of what’s worth doing, what needs a closer look, and what to leave alone.
